ABS Clearing House ***

Why:  this is the only working example of the thing BBNJ is about to build. Article 12 sets up a notification chain: a researcher notifies before collecting marine genetic resources, the system generates a standardized batch identifier, the researcher notifies again after collection, and again when the resources are used. That identifier threads the whole chain together so a benefit-sharing obligation can be traced from a cruise to a sample to a sequence to a patent. WDPA gives you a template for area records. The ABS Clearing-House gives you the template for the MGR notification chain, which is the harder half of the schema because it involves linked records and legal consequence rather than a single flat description.

Access and Benefit-Sharing Clearing-House (ABS Clearing-House)
 Operated by the Secretariat of the Convention on Biological Diversity (SCBD)
 https://absch.cbd.int/
 Global platform for exchanging access and benefit-sharing information under the Nagoya Protocol, including national ABS measures, competent authorities, permits, and the Internationally Recognized Certificate of Compliance.

Narrative
 The ABS Clearing-House was established by Article 14 of the Nagoya Protocol and became operational alongside the Protocol's entry into force in 2014. It is run by the Secretariat of the Convention on Biological Diversity in Montreal, Canada, as part of the wider CBD clearing-house mechanism. Its function is to make national access and benefit-sharing rules legible across borders: it publishes each Party's ABS legislation, its designated national focal points and competent national authorities, model contractual clauses, and the permits issued for access to genetic resources. Its most consequential feature is automatic: when a Party publishes a permit or its equivalent, the system generates an Internationally Recognized Certificate of Compliance carrying a unique identifier, which serves as evidence that the genetic resource was accessed with prior informed consent and on mutually agreed terms. The first IRCC was issued by India on 1 October 2015. Records are submitted through standardized common formats by validated national publishing authorities, with mandatory fields marked and a confidentiality flag available for restricted content, and submission is supported across the six official UN languages. For a BBNJ audience the relevance is structural rather than geographic. The Nagoya Protocol covers genetic resources under national jurisdiction, so this system contains nothing from areas beyond national jurisdiction, and it is not a source of ocean data. What it offers is a decade of operating experience with the exact mechanism BBNJ Article 12 describes: a permit-triggered, auto-generated, uniquely identified certificate that links a collection event to downstream utilization and can be verified at checkpoints. Total record counts grow continuously with ratifications and are not usefully quoted as a fixed figure.

Write / contribution access

National publishing authority submission
 Submit | Validated Authority Publication
 Only designated national authorities may publish. Each Party nominates a publishing authority whose credentials are validated by the Secretariat. The authority submits records using the common formats; the Secretariat performs a completeness check rather than a substantive legal review. When a permit record is published, the IRCC is generated automatically rather than being separately authored, which is the mechanism worth studying for Article 12.
 https://absch.cbd.int/
 Source: ABS Clearing-House

Confidential content handling
 Submit | Restricted Field Flagging
 Submitters may mark specified fields as confidential, which withholds them from public display while retaining them in the record. This is the existing precedent for the confidentiality carve-outs the BBNJ COP has not yet defined.
